The two-channel count emission takes one covariate per channel, and every seam
above it passes one — including the Rust backend (#658).

**The families.** #631 refused a covariate on a pair family because "the two
channels would each need their own — an exposure for the total, a trial count
for the successes — and one tensor cannot be both". It can, with the channel
axis the observation already has: `(..., 2)`, splitting where the observation
splits. A covariate without that axis is still refused. **The neutral covariate
is not ones**: it is ones for the total, whose exposure multiplies a rate, and
the family's own declared `trials` for the successes, whose covariate replaces a
trial count — ones on both asks a beta-binomial for more successes than trials
and scores `-inf`.

**The seams.** `external_field` and `marginal_log_likelihood_torch` scored
without one. `external_field` was a defect rather than deferred work:
`fit_spatio_sequential` passes it a posterior computed *with* the covariate
while its own `log_density` scored *without*, so the two halves of one step
disagreed about the model — monotone either way, which is why nothing failed.
`covariate_block` is the one slice all three share, and it adds the trailing
singleton only where the covariate carries no axes of its own.

**The Rust backend.** The kernel tabulated `log p(count | class, state)` and
indexed by the count; under a covariate the density is a function of the count
*and* what it is scored against, so no table indexed by the count alone exists.
The kernel never knew what the index meant, only that the table was built from
it, so the caller tabulates the distinct `(count, covariate)` pairs and hands
the row each observation falls in. `src/coupled.rs` changes in one way only:
the index widens from `u16` to `u32`, since a row is no longer bounded by the
largest count. Without a covariate the rows *are* the counts and the table is
count-major — the same numbers as before, asserted on the arrays.

At the ci instance the tabulation finds 9,989 and 245 rows against 64,000
observations, so a per-vertex exposure repeated down the positions stays a
table rather than becoming a per-observation score array. Agreement with the
NumPy oracle under a covariate: 2.7e-15 relative on the evidence, 2.9e-15 on
the field, 1.0e-09 absolute on the posteriors, one thread.

**A varying kernel reaches a fit** (#658). #656 gave the three evaluators a
`(T - 1, K, K)` kernel; nothing that *fits* took one. `baum_welch_family` now
does, and `SpatioSequentialParams.self_transition` takes the kernel itself: a
scalar rate as before, a `(K, K)` matrix for the chain, or `(S - 1, K, K)` per
transition. The matrix forms are not required to be circulant, which is the
point of them — a kernel assembled from parts, a base over one latent times a
kernel over another, is not a circulant at any rate, so the scalar could not
express one **even where it does not vary along the chain**. How a caller
assembled a kernel is the caller's; this carries the result and does not
reconstruct it. A per-step circulant *rate* is conserved in
`sandbox.circulant_schedule`, superseded in capability and refereeing the
circulant case bitwise.

**A per-step kernel is conditioned on, not fitted.** It carries
`(T - 1) * K * (K - 1)` free values against `T - 1` transitions per sequence,
so at the sequence counts this repository runs it is not identifiable, and an M
step that re-estimated it would return the posterior it was handed. Told one,
`baum_welch_family` holds it and fits the initial distribution and the
emissions; the spatial M step does the same with a per-step rate. Told a single
matrix, both fit it exactly as before. That is the standing a covariate has,
and it is asserted both ways so neither half can quietly change.

Over 400 positions whose stickiness falls from 0.95 to 0.55, a fit told the
kernel reaches −340.422 against the −345.477 of one free to fit any single
matrix. The margin grows with the chain: +1.0 at 300 positions, +5.1 at 400,
+7.4 at 500.

**A family whose observation has its own axes can now be fitted** (#658).
`baum_welch_family` unpacked `n_sequences, length = data.shape` — the whole
shape, not its leading two — so it refused outright every family whose
observation carries axes of its own, a family over a pair of counts among
them. The families were made to condition on a covariate and no fit could tell
one: a downstream consumer measured `ValueError: too many values to unpack` on
this branch and reported it. It now reads the leading two axes and leaves what
follows to the family, as every other seam does.

Two draws had the same defect at one remove: `NegativeBinomialEmission.sample`
and `BetaBinomialEmission.sample` flattened the covariate with `reshape(-1)`,
which assumed `states` was one-dimensional and broadcast against nothing at
two. They reshape to `states`' own shape.

Refereed: a pair family fits at all, which fails differently from a recovery
failure and so is asserted on its own; and over an exposure spanning a factor
of 9, told it the fit recovers a planted 3.0 and 12.0 to 15%, where the same
fit on the same data not told it misses by more than 25%.

**The simulator draws under the covariate**, so a planted instance exists for a
covariate-conditioned fit to recover. The covariate is selected by the same
nodes and flattened the same way as the states the draw runs over.

**The first tabulation was slower than the oracle it replaces**, and this is
the number worth carrying. Tabulating the *distinct* `(count, covariate)` pairs
is the smallest exact table, found by one `np.unique` over an `(S·V, 2)` array
per call — **135.6 ms of a 141.4 ms E step**, putting the compiled backend at
**0.6x** the NumPy path it exists to beat. Factorizing the covariate alone and
addressing the outer product arithmetically gives **12.4 ms, 6.5x** the oracle,
against **37.2x** uncovaried. The table is larger and never sorted; fewer rows
was the wrong thing to optimize.

Root `CLAUDE.md` gains the rule that makes that finding a rule rather than an
anecdote: a ratio is half the case and the effect size is the other half, since
a ratio alone admits a 10x on a term worth 40 us. `likelihood/CLAUDE.md` gains
two: a kernel's table is indexed by a row and not a value, so widening what it
can score is a question about the caller's index; and where a table is built
per call, the build is the measurement.

`docs/tex/textbook.tex` states both additions to the coupled model: an emission
scored against a per-observation covariate, one per channel where the
observation has channels, and a chain kernel that varies with position, which
is conditioned on rather than estimated.
